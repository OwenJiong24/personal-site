**Conclusion**: advertising does not buy sales, it **amplifies something already verified**. Fail any of the three tests below and ads will scale your loss with great precision.

## Test 1 — does the unit economics pass

Every paid order must carry its acquisition cost. Compute three numbers first (the [pricing calculator](/tools/pricing-calculator/) produces the first two):

| Number | How | Why it comes first |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin per piece | price − (material + labour + failure rate + shipping + returns + platform fees) | If margin is zero, ads are a pure loss |
| Maximum affordable CAC | gross margin × 0.5 (allowing the first order to lose half, expecting a repeat to repay it) | Stop above this line |
| Repeat / list value | what share of buyers comes back | The only thing that justifies losing money on order one |

One judgement: **a piece with margin under 30% of price should not be advertised**. Fix pricing and cost first — most makers are underpaid on their own hours, not under-exposed.

## Test 2 — do you already have a photo that sells

Ads buy click and conversion efficiency, and efficiency lives almost entirely in the creative.

- A winning creative looks like this: in **organic** traffic one main photo already clicks and converts clearly better than the rest.
- Launching ads without one means paying to test a hundred photos — the most expensive way to learn what a photo does.
- The cheap order: **test photos on organic traffic (free) → pick the winner → pay to scale it**.

Engineering note from the assistant: the creative is the only asset you can carry. Accounts, keywords and audiences all move with platform rules; **a photo and a caption that have worked travel with you to any channel**.

## Test 3 — can you read four numbers

Dashboards offer dozens. At small budgets watch four and ignore the rest:

1. **CPM** (cost per mille): absurdly high means the audience is too broad.
2. **CTR** (click-through rate): under 0.8% is usually a photo problem, not a bid problem.
3. **CPC** (cost per click): compare with margin — CPC above margin ÷ expected conversion means stop.
4. **Orders**, not the ROI report: under about ten orders, "return on ad spend" has no statistical meaning.

Minimum viable habit: record three columns a day — **spend, clicks, orders**. After two weeks you will see a pattern, and it beats any dashboard.

## How to spend $10 a day: a 14-day structure

| Days | Do | Stop line |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | One winning photo, 3–5 narrow long-tail phrases, low bid | CPM is absurd (audience too broad) → change phrases |
| 4–10 | Keep only what runs, log the three columns daily | Three consecutive days with zero orders → stop |
| 11–14 | Keep one winner, raise budget 20% and watch orders | Orders rise with spend → demand is real, keep scaling |

The point of this structure is **not profit; it is buying one answer**: does this piece survive paid traffic. Once you have the answer, scaling has a meaning.

## Three kinds of traffic not to buy

1. **Broad head terms** ("gifts", "handmade jewelry"): comparison shoppers, near-zero conversion; the platform keeps the money.
2. **Boosted posts**: buys vanity metrics, produces no orders.
3. **"Targeted buyer packages"** from unknown vendors: the handmade ad pool is one of the most heavily farmed places on the internet. Anything promising guaranteed impressions or guaranteed enquiries is a fee extraction.

## The judgement: ads are leverage, not the engine

> "In essence we gather people with the same need together, so we do not have to advertise to find orders — the orders find us."
> — from OJ's own notes (redacted)

Read it backwards and you get the boundary of advertising: **ads accelerate "the orders find us", and that requires a "us" to exist first**. The site, the product line and the email list are the engine; ads are the lever. With no engine, a longer lever is only more dangerous.
