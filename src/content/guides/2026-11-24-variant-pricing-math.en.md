## Conclusion: one 55-dollar price gives the small variant 28.46 dollars of contribution and the large one 6.96

**Charging the same price for every size looks fair and behaves like a cross-subsidy. Using figures already published on this site, a 55-dollar price leaves the small version with **28.46 dollars** of contribution and the large version with **6.96** — a **4.09 times** gap, even though the customer sees one product with three options.**

## The worked table

Inputs, all stated: hourly rate **$25**, fee **6.5%**, fixed charges **$0.97** per order. The three variants are examples — small **$12 material, 0.4 h**, medium **$18, 0.5 h**, large **$26, 0.7 h**. Cost per unit = material + hours × rate + fixed charges.

| Variant | Cost | At a flat $55: contribution | At cost × 2: price | Contribution | Contribution rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small | $22.97 | **$28.46** | $45.94 | $19.98 | 43.5% |
| Medium | $31.47 | $19.96 | $62.94 | $27.38 | 43.5% |
| Large | $44.47 | **$6.96** | $88.94 | $38.69 | 43.5% |

**Two things to read.** First, at a flat price the large variant contributes less than a quarter of the small one, while taking longer to make and tying up more material — see [the cash-flow gap](/guides/cash-flow-gap/) for why that matters before settlement. Second, multiplying each variant's cost by two produces the **same 43.5% contribution rate** at every size, because the margin is set as a ratio of cost rather than a fixed number.

## Why the flat price feels right and is wrong

Sellers choose one price per variant for a sensible reason: a catalogue with three prices looks complicated, and customers compare the options directly. But the comparison runs in the direction of the cheapest option — the buyer sees the large one as "more for the same money", which is true, and the seller pays for it. Keeping one price is a deliberate subsidy to the largest variant, not a neutral simplification.

The alternative is not necessarily three unrelated prices. It is to decide what the price ladder should be:

| Pricing rule | What it gives you | What it risks |
|---|---|---|
| Same price for all | Simple catalogue | Large variant earns $6.96 |
| Cost × 2 per variant | Equal margin at every size | Large price may be above what buyers pay |
| Fixed gap between sizes | Predictable ladder | Still needs a floor check on the largest |

## Variants also change what you hold

A price ladder is only half the decision. Three sizes usually mean three sets of material, three minimum order quantities and three shelves of unsold stock. The large variant that earns 6.96 dollars at a flat price is also the one that ties up the most cash per unit — the same pattern [the cash-flow gap](/guides/cash-flow-gap/) describes, just multiplied by the number of variants. Before adding a size, ask whether you will sell enough of it to turn that stock within the gap you can afford. If the answer is no, the variant is not a pricing problem; it is a working-capital problem that a price change will not fix.

## The check before you list variants

1. Compute cost per variant — material, hours at your rate, fixed charges. Use the [pricing calculator](/tools/pricing-calculator/) for the cost base.
2. For each planned price, compute contribution = price × (1 − fee) − cost.
3. Compare the contribution of the smallest and largest. If it is more than two times, you are choosing to subsidise one of them; make that choice explicitly.
4. Decide which variant you actually want to sell more of, and set the ladder to make it true.
